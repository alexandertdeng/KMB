CCHS at a glance

Congenital Central Hypoventilation Syndrome is an ultra-rare genetic condition, caused by a mutation in the PHOX2B gene, that disrupts the brain’s automatic control of breathing. When a child with CCHS falls asleep, their body simply forgets to breathe. CCHS is a suspected cause of some cases of sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS), or cot death.

Every night of their lives, they depend on mechanical ventilation, through a tracheostomy or a mask, to keep them alive until morning.

Also known asOndine’s curse, a failure of the body’s drive to breathe, because it cannot sense oxygen and carbon dioxide levels.
Life supportLifelong ventilation every time they sleep, and for some children, when they are awake too.
Not just breathingCCHS is not just a respiratory disease. 50% of children with specific types of CCHS are born with or go on to develop tumours such as neuroblastoma, a type of cancer.
Treatment todayNone. There is no cure and no therapeutic option beyond artificial life support. That is what we are here to change.

About CCHS: not just a respiratory disease

CCHS is rare, but more common than once thought. Recent registry data put birth prevalence at 1 in 67,400 in South Korea and 1 in 105,334 in France. Based on these figures, a forthcoming analysis by Rand et al. estimates that 11,733 to 39,602 people aged 21 or under are living with CCHS worldwide, far more than registries currently count. Some sudden unexplained infant deaths may also be caused by undiagnosed CCHS. Genetic testing to confirm CCHS only became available in 2003.

  • CCHS is caused by a genetic mutation affecting a particular gene named PHOX2B. This gene is responsible for the development of nerves early in pregnancy to form specific types of nerve cells, especially in the autonomic nervous system. As the nerve cells do not work as they should, messages between the body and brain are not passed on correctly.
  • CCHS can come with additional consequences. Approximately 20 per cent of children with CCHS will also have a condition named Hirschsprung’s Disease. About 50% of people with an NPARM type of CCHS have or go on to develop neural crest tumours. Many children with CCHS have gut issues, eyesight problems or poor blood sugar regulation, and some need a cardiac pacemaker because their heart can pause. A screening checklist by genotype is below.
  • For most people the breathing problem happens only during sleep. In severe CCHS it happens awake too, and may be most noticeable when feeding (particularly in infancy) or when concentrating.

Ventilatory support is life support for patients with CCHS.

Genetics and inheritance

  • CCHS is a dominant genetic condition, meaning only one PHOX2B gene needs to contain a mutation to result in the phenotypic presentation of CCHS.
  • Although most genetic diseases are inherited from parents, the majority of CCHS cases are spontaneous in nature. The rate of inheritance of CCHS from a parent who has CCHS is believed to be 50%.
  • Mosaic parents (who may have some ‘normal’ PHOX2B gene cells and some abnormal but are typically asymptomatic or have a much milder version of CCHS) have been identified within the CCHS population, but these are still rare. Parents who wish to have additional children after having a child with CCHS are encouraged to seek genetic counselling.
  • PHOX2B mutations are stable in transmission from one generation to the next, but penetrance and phenotype can still vary significantly. Along with early recognition of the clinical features of CCHS, the gold standard test to diagnose CCHS is genetic testing to identify mutations in the PHOX2B gene, including PARMs, NPARMs, or deletions and duplications.

Types of ventilation support

  • Most CCHS patients are ventilated through a tracheostomy, and it is often hospitals’ first choice. Other types of ventilation, such as mask ventilation, can be successful even in early infancy (see the published evidence and GOSH’s guidance). The chart below compares the options, and our page on mask vs tracheostomy vs pacer goes into more detail.
  • The right choice depends on a range of factors including the severity of your or your child’s phenotype. It is important to consider the advantages and disadvantages of each type of ventilation support including secondary factors. For example, a tracheostomy can affect speech and smell, while mask ventilation can increase the risk of midfacial hypoplasia. Phrenic nerve pacing is not offered in every country or health system; for example, it is not yet available on the NHS.
  • Different hospitals have different protocols with CCHS patients. Some favour certain ventilation options more than others.
